======================================================================
FINAL @merry CALIBRATION RULES
======================================================================

DO NOT OVER-EXPLAIN THE IDEA VISUALLY.

If the concept is understandable in one glance, it may be too literal.

The viewer should be able to sense that a rigorous system is operating without necessarily being able to immediately name it.

Do not turn every mathematical or scientific concept into a classroom demonstration.

Mystery is allowed.

STRUCTURAL LEGIBILITY MATTERS MORE THAN SYMBOLIC LEGIBILITY.

The system should have consequences that can be seen in the body, material, or motion.

The audience does not need labels, equations, arrows, annotations, diagrams, or visual proof that you researched the concept.

The artwork should function first as a strange object or organism.

DO NOT MAKE EVERYTHING PERFECTLY SYMMETRICAL.

Symmetry is useful only when the governing system actually calls for it.

Symmetry breaking is often more interesting.

Allow:
failed repetition,
almost-symmetry,
mutation between repeated structures,
local defects,
uneven growth,
developmental accidents,
and competing organizations.

Perfect mirror symmetry can quickly make a result feel decorative or logo-like.

DO NOT DEFAULT TO CENTERED POSTER COMPOSITION.

The image can feel like a frame accidentally captured from a real event.

Allow:
awkward crops,
partial anatomy leaving the frame,
negative space in strange places,
off-center creatures,
structures entering from outside the image,
and compositions that feel discovered rather than carefully arranged for a poster.

But the subject should remain visually readable enough that we can study what happened.

DO NOT GIVE EVERYTHING A CLEAN SILHOUETTE.

Forms may overlap.
Appendages may merge into other structures.
The edge of the body may become uncertain.
One structure may visually interrupt another.

A complicated organism does not need to read like a character-design turnaround.

MATERIALS SHOULD SOMETIMES DISAGREE WITH FORM.

An impossible mathematical structure does not need an appropriately futuristic material.

Make impossible geometry out of foam latex.

Make microscopic structures look hand-painted.

Make something topologically impossible wobble like rubber.

Make an alien developmental process appear to have been manufactured with 1982 prop-shop materials.

The collision between advanced ideas and primitive physical fabrication is part of the appeal.

DO NOT CONFUSE PSYCHEDELIC WITH RANDOMNESS.

Colorful chaos is not enough.

The system underneath should still have rules.

The best result is:
strict underlying logic + visually insane consequences.

Not:
random colorful stuff everywhere.

Avoid meaningless swirls, generic kaleidoscope effects, mandalas, rainbow smoke, generic trippy backgrounds, and arbitrary distortion unless the chosen system actually produces them.

DO NOT MAKE THE WORK TOO ELEGANT.

Elegance is welcome when the mathematics creates it naturally, but avoid polishing every result into museum-grade generative design.

Some of @merry’s strongest results should feel:
overgrown,
slightly wrong,
overbuilt,
biologically excessive,
cheaply manufactured,
unstable,
or one evolutionary decision away from disaster.

ALLOW COMPETING READINGS.

A successful form can be difficult to classify.

A viewer might ask:

Is that an eye or a hole?
Is that a limb or a surface?
Is that flesh or rubber?
Is that one creature or several?
Is that anatomical motion or tape corruption?
Is that structure intentional or a generative accident?

Do not resolve every question.

TEMPORAL COHERENCE IS NOT THE SAME AS STABILITY.

For animation, the creature should feel like the same transforming system from frame to frame, but individual features do not need to remain perfectly stable.

Small identity drift, anatomical instability, temporal duplication, or generative flicker can become part of the transformation.

Do not aggressively stabilize away all weirdness.

However, avoid meaningless total reset between frames.

The deformation should feel cumulative.

WHAT HAPPENS EARLY SHOULD AFFECT WHAT HAPPENS LATER.

Transformation should have memory.

If a structure splits, later motion should inherit the split.
If something grows, later frames should respond to that growth.
If the face folds into a new topology, do not casually return it to normal in the next moment.

Treat the animation as a process, not a sequence of unrelated hallucinations.

USE RESTRAINT AROUND "EPIC."

Avoid automatically adding:
cinematic explosions,
dramatic smoke,
god rays,
huge landscapes,
epic establishing shots,
trailer lighting,
hero poses,
slow-motion reveals,
or blockbuster spectacle.

The strange event itself is enough.

A static camera looking at one impossible thing happening in a shitty room can be far more effective.

SMALL CAN BE WEIRDER THAN HUGE.

Do not always escalate scale.

A tiny change to one eye, tooth, fingertip, pore, strand of hair, or fold of skin can be more disturbing than transforming the entire universe.

Let concepts operate at whatever scale creates the strangest consequence.

THE ENVIRONMENT DOES NOT NEED TO BE INTERESTING.

Do not compete with the creature unnecessarily.

Plain rooms, dark studio sets, crude laboratory spaces, blank practical-effects stages, or ambiguous physical backgrounds are often useful because they make the impossible structure easier to see.

If the environment transforms, it should be because the governing system requires it.

DO NOT "BEAUTIFY" THE REFERENCE SUBJECT BACK INTO A GENERIC AI WOMAN.

When using @merry:
preserve her specific visual identity where possible,
including her recognizable facial relationships and vivid pink hair,
but allow those traits to become raw structural material.

Do not replace her with:
a generic fashion model,
a younger-looking generic woman,
a hyper-glamorous AI face,
perfect poreless skin,
or idealized fantasy beauty.

Recognizability should come from HER, not from a generic approximation of "pretty pink-haired woman."

MOST IMPORTANT FAILURE CHECK:

If the result could be described as:

"a woman with mathematical patterns on her,"
"a psychedelic woman,"
"a sci-fi creature with fractals,"
"a colorful monster,"
or
"a woman with glowing geometry,"

then the transformation is probably too shallow.

Push the system deeper into anatomy, physics, material, and causality.

The desired reaction is closer to:

"I don't know what the fuck that thing is, but I can tell something very specific is governing it."
